PROMPT: Fix Critical Math Bugs & Calculation Errors in Project Path Pro Forma
This is a follow-up to the previous prompt. The design and narrative changes are in progress — this prompt focuses ONLY on math bugs, calculation errors, and data integrity issues that make the pro forma indefensible. These must be fixed before anything else.
BUG 1 (CRITICAL): Investment Calculated on Active/Adopted Providers Instead of Licensed Providers
Current behavior: The 3-Year Financial Summary table calculates investment cost using licensed providers × adoption rate × price × 12. This means:

Year 1: 50 × 20% × $120 × 12 = $14,400 — but shows $72K (uses full 50) ✓
Year 2: 230 × 65% × $120 × 12 = $215,280 — shows $212K ✓ (BUG — using adopted count)
Year 3: 500 × 100% × $120 × 12 = $720,000 — shows $542K (BUG — using adopted count ~377)
Total shown: $827K
Correct behavior: Investment = licensed/contracted providers × price × 12. You pay for licenses whether providers adopt or not. Adoption affects VALUE, never COST. CFOs know their contract cost — if the model shows less than what they're paying, they'll immediately distrust everything else.
Correct calculation:
Year 1: 50 × $120 × 12 = $72,000
Year 2: 230 × $120 × 12 = $331,200
Year 3: 500 × $120 × 12 = $720,000
Total: $1,123,200
Find the investment calculation function and change it to use the licensed provider count for each year (the rollout numbers: 50, 230, 500), NOT the active/adopted provider count. Do NOT multiply by adoption/utilization rate when calculating cost.
This will cascade into these KPIs which must also update:
Value-to-Cost ratio: was 6.6x → should become ~4.8x
Simple ROI: was 557% → should become ~382%
Net Value: was $4.6M → should become ~$4.28M
Payback Period: will increase (which is MORE credible)
IRR: will change
Sensitivity analysis: all three scenarios' ROI and payback will shift
Also fix for all pricing modes:
Per Unit/Month: investment = licensed providers × price × 12 (NOT adopted)
Annual License: investment = annual fee (fixed, no change needed)
Per Encounter: investment = total encounters × price per encounter (here adoption DOES correctly affect cost since you only pay per encounter used — this mode is fine)


BUG 2 (CRITICAL): Per-Provider Value Uses Pilot Count, Not Scale Count
Current behavior: The KPI card shows "Annual Value at Scale: $3.8M" with "per provider: $75K". The $75K is calculated as $3.8M / 50 (Year 1 pilot providers).
Problem: This is massively misleading. A reader sees "$75K per provider" and thinks each of their 500 providers generates $75K. The real number at full scale is $3.8M / 500 = $7,600 per provider.
Fix: The "per provider" metric should use the FULL SCALE provider count (the Year 3 / max rollout number), not the Year 1 pilot count. Display:

"Per provider at scale: $7.6K/year"
If you want to also show the pilot economics, add it as a secondary line:
"Per pilot provider (Year 1): $75K/year"
But the PRIMARY per-provider number must use the scale count. This is what a CFO will use to evaluate the deal.

BUG 4 (HIGH): IRR Front-Loads All Costs Into Period 0
Current behavior: The methodology states "Period 0 is the total cost basis (implementation fees plus full contract subscription)." This means ALL 3 years of cost ($1.12M corrected) is loaded into Year 0, with value flowing in Years 1-3.
Problem: This is not how real cash flows work. You pay Year 1 costs in Year 1, Year 2 costs in Year 2, etc. Front-loading all costs into Period 0 artificially inflates IRR because it treats the investment as a single upfront spend with all returns coming later.
Fix: Use proper year-by-year net cash flows for IRR:

Period 0 (or Year 0): Implementation fees only (if any). If no implementation fee, Period 0 = $0 or start from Year 1.
Year 1: Value Year 1 - Cost Year 1 = $266K - $72K = $194K
Year 2: Value Year 2 - Cost Year 2 = $1.4M - $331K = $1.07M
Year 3: Value Year 3 - Cost Year 3 = $3.8M - $720K = $3.08M
If there IS an implementation fee, that goes in Period 0 as the initial outlay, and Years 1-3 are net cash flows (value minus subscription cost).
The IRR will still be strong with proper cash flows — but it will be a real, defensible number.
